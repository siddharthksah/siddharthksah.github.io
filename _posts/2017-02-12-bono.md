Last week at Quark, on the library lawns, a whiteboard drew the Batman logo on its own while people ate breakfast around it. Behind it were two [stepper motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepper_motor), two cords, a pen in a 3D printed carriage, and a laptop. The machine is called Bono. It's our version of the [polargraph](http://www.polargraph.co.uk/), a wall-drawing robot, and this post is about how it works and what we learned building it.

![Bono at the Quark booth on the library lawns: whiteboard on a stand, cords running from corner pulleys to the pen gondola, the laptop driving it](/images/posts/bono/quark-lawns.jpg){: srcset="/images/posts/bono/quark-lawns-480.jpg 480w, /images/posts/bono/quark-lawns-960.jpg 960w, /images/posts/bono/quark-lawns.jpg 1594w" sizes="(max-width: 800px) 92vw, 770px"}

## How a polargraph works

A polargraph has no rails. Two motors sit at the top corners of the board, each winding a cord, and the pen carriage (the gondola) hangs where the two cords meet. That's the whole mechanism. The two cord lengths fix the gondola's position the same way two compass arcs fix a point. So the machine's native coordinates are cord lengths, and X and Y only exist in software.

That makes the [inverse kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_kinematics) the main problem. Each cord length is the hypotenuse of a triangle between its motor and the pen, so drawing a straight line means both motors have to follow speed profiles that change continuously and aren't linear. The [Makelangelo firmware](https://github.com/MarginallyClever/Makelangelo-firmware) handles it the way most plotter firmware does. It chops every line into short segments and re-solves the triangles at each one.

Gravity does two jobs here. It keeps both cords taut, since a motor can wind a cord but can't push one, and it pulls the gondola down into the V. It also swings the gondola like a [pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) if the motors accelerate faster than the pendulum's [natural frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_frequency) can follow. That's why the acceleration setting matters more than any other number in the firmware.

## Counterweights and the usable drawing area

The cords carry [counterweights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterweight), and ours are water bottles. We use water bottles for a lot of things. They keep the slack side of each cord under tension so the printed ball-chain pulleys can't skip, and the water level is adjustable: top up for coarse changes, pour some out for fine ones. Half full worked best. Working that out took an afternoon.

![Bono halfway through the Batman logo on paper taped to the whiteboard, counterweight bottles hanging on both cords](/images/posts/bono/batman.jpg){: srcset="/images/posts/bono/batman-480.jpg 480w, /images/posts/bono/batman.jpg 820w" sizes="(max-width: 800px) 92vw, 770px" loading="lazy" decoding="async"}

The usable drawing area is smaller than the board. Near the top corners one cord runs nearly horizontal, and a horizontal cord needs a lot of tension to hold any weight, so the gondola wobbles there. Near the bottom the V gets wide and shallow and position resolution drops. The area that draws well is the middle band, roughly the middle sixty percent of the board, and we placed every image there.

## Electronics and software

Our expense sheet shows how the electronics went. The first driver purchase is an L293D shield. The second, eleven days later, is a [RAMPS 1.4](https://reprap.org/wiki/RAMPS_1.4) with proper stepper drivers. The upgrade bought two things: current limiting, so the motors stopped overheating at idle, and sixteenth-microstepping. With full steps every curve had a visible scallop, because the pen traced the motor's [cogging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cogging_torque) one step at a time. Microstepping removed it. The final stack is two NEMA 17 steppers, the RAMPS board on an [Arduino Mega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arduino), an [SMPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switched-mode_power_supply) with cooling fans, and printed mounts for all of it. Total cost was ₹20,372, and the 3D printing again cost more than the electronics.

On the software side, the [Polargraph controller](http://www.polargraph.co.uk/) does what the Quark crowd saw: import a bitmap, click draw. It maps pixel brightness to shading density, so dark regions get tight scribbles and light regions get sparse ones, and a [servo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servo_(radio_control)) on the gondola lifts the pen between strokes. A whiteboard marker draws all of it. That was the pitch: notice boards and lecture diagrams drawn by a machine whose only consumable costs thirty rupees.

## What we learned

1. Gravity tensions the cords for free, and it also swings the gondola. The fix for the swing is the acceleration cap in the firmware. There's no feedback loop to tune, so PID doesn't apply.
2. Scalloped curves were full-step cogging. Sixteenth-microstepping on real stepper drivers fixed what pen pressure couldn't.
3. The top corners wobble. Keep the drawing inside the middle sixty percent of the board and the output looks much more precise.
4. Water bottles beat fixed counterweights because you can adjust the mass in ten-gram sips.
5. A robot drawing Batman draws a crowd. A robot drawing calibration grids doesn't. Pick the demo for the audience.
6. Buying the wrong motor driver and then the right one eleven days later looks like waste on the expense sheet. It was the fastest way to learn what we needed. The ₹20,372 sheet is the most useful project log we have.
