We built a submarine out of plumbing. It is called Vandubbi, it is a wireless [remotely operated underwater vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remotely_operated_underwater_vehicle) with a live video feed, and we exhibited it at Quark, our campus technical festival, this week. This post is the build story while it is still fresh.

![Top view of the Vandubbi frame: a PVC chassis with four brushless thrusters at the corners, the Arduino, power distribution board, and ESCs mounted in the middle](/images/posts/vandubbi/top.jpg)

The chassis is PVC pipe. That is a deliberate choice rather than a compromise: PVC is cheap, strong for its weight, naturally buoyant, easy to cut with hand tools in a hostel room, and when a design turns out wrong, the failed version becomes spare parts for the next one. The [SeaPerch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SeaPerch) build manual was our starting point, and by the second frame we had outgrown it into our own design.

Thrust comes from brushless motors at the corners, driven through [ESCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_speed_control) with propellers and motor mounts we 3D printed ourselves, along with printed caps for waterproofing the motors. An Arduino reads the radio receiver channels and drives everything. Where the printed parts came from is a story of its own: parametric propeller designs from the open hardware community, resized and reprinted until they pushed water instead of just stirring it.

## The numbers

Vandubbi V1 runs for 20 minutes on a charge of its [LiPo batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_polymer_battery) in still water and carries a payload of up to 4 kg. The radio link has a theoretical range of over a mile in line of sight, and we have tested it successfully to 300 meters. The [FPV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-person_view_(radio_control)) set transmits live video on 5.8 GHz to a headset, alongside telemetry for acceleration, velocity, and battery status. A focused LED on the nose keeps the camera useful when the water gets dark.

## Buoyancy is the actual engineering

The glamorous problems in a submarine are propulsion and video. [Buoyancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy) is the problem that fills the evenings, because a payload that can vary by 4 kg will happily flip a small vehicle that was trimmed for zero.

Our answer is bottles. Sealed bottles mounted at the four corners of the frame set the buoyant force, and moving them adjusts trim to match whatever the payload does to the balance. The frame geometry itself provides passive roll stabilization, so the bot rights itself without any code spending battery to do it. We simulated the flow before committing to the frame shape, then trusted the pool tests more than the simulation.

![Three-quarter view of the assembled PVC frame with a corner thruster, ESCs, and wiring visible](/images/posts/vandubbi/frame.jpg)

## The failsafe involves no code

My favorite design decision is the simplest one. The vehicle is trimmed slightly positive, so if anything fails (a wire, a battery, an ESC, the radio link), the thrusters stop and Vandubbi floats to the surface on physics alone. A failsafe that needs working electronics to save you from failed electronics is a contradiction; buoyancy has no firmware to crash.

## Water versus radio

An honest note on the word wireless. Radio and water are enemies, and both our control link and the 5.8 GHz video are line-of-sight tools, so wireless operation in practice means shallow running with the antennas thought through. That constraint is baked into what Vandubbi is for: surveillance and inspection near the surface, where the expensive commercial options are absurd overkill.

## What it cost

V1 was entirely self-funded by the team. Our pitch for V2 lists the full bill of materials at ₹34,850, most of it the FPV set, the 3D printed parts, and the radio gear, and includes the sentence "funds will eliminate the key Jugaad which is often the reason of fail." I stand by that sentence more with every pool test.

The codes and the 3D printable files for V1 are open source in the public domain. Version two is already on the drawing board, and it will be better, because we now know exactly which parts of version one were held together by tape and optimism.
