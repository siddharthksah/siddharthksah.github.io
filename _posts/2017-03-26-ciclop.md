Last month at Quark our table held three machines: [the submarine](https://siddharthksah.github.io/posts/2017/02/vandubbi/), [the wall-drawing plotter](https://siddharthksah.github.io/posts/2017/02/bono/), and a [3D scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_scanning) built mostly out of 3D printed parts. This post is about the scanner, and mostly about the part the kit documentation skips, which is everything after assembly.

![Our Quark booth: the scanner with its checkerboard calibration target on a white tablecloth, the handwritten spec poster on the wall](/images/posts/ciclop/booth.jpg){: srcset="/images/posts/ciclop/booth-480.jpg 480w, /images/posts/ciclop/booth.jpg 868w" sizes="(max-width: 800px) 92vw, 770px"}

The handwritten poster claims 0.5 mm resolution, two to three minutes per scan, a 250 x 250 x 205 mm scanning volume, and scanning in the dark. All of those held up with a festival crowd poking at the machine. The last one is my favorite, for reasons below.

## Why build a scanner

The Aerodynamics Club printer will make any part you can model. The bottleneck is the modeling, because most of what you want to copy already exists as an object on your desk. A scanner closes that gap: digitize the thing, edit the mesh, print the edit.

We picked [Ciclop](https://reprap.org/wiki/ciclop), BQ's open-source laser scanner (files released under [Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/)), over Atlas and FabScan, after reading a lot of scanner documentation. FabScan's bachelor thesis is good reading. The design is printed parts and M8 threaded rod, an [Arduino UNO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arduino) under a ZUM shield, one [stepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepper_motor) on a turntable, a Logitech webcam, and two line lasers that cost one hundred rupees.

## How laser triangulation works

The principle is [triangulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangulation_(computer_vision)). A [line laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_line_level) projects a vertical plane of light. The webcam watches from a known angle. Wherever the line lands on the object, its sideways displacement in the image encodes depth, one slice per exposure. The turntable rotates the object through a known step angle between slices, and because the rotation is commanded, registration comes free. Every slice already knows its angle, so no alignment algorithm runs.

![The scanner mid-scan in the hostel room: red laser line striping a shampoo bottle on the turntable, the ZUM board glowing in the printed tower](/images/posts/ciclop/scanner-live.jpg){: srcset="/images/posts/ciclop/scanner-live-480.jpg 480w, /images/posts/ciclop/scanner-live.jpg 660w" sizes="(max-width: 800px) 92vw, 770px" loading="lazy" decoding="async"}

The capture software is [Horus](https://github.com/bqlabs/horus), BQ's open-source companion. It drives the board, streams the webcam, and assembles the [point cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_cloud) live on screen. Watching the first cloud take the shape of a shampoo bottle was a good moment.

## Getting usable scans

Assembly is an afternoon. Getting scans worth keeping took months, and that was the real work.

The webcam is the first problem. A stock Logitech runs autoexposure and autofocus, and both are bad for laser detection. The exposure changes with the room, the laser line blooms and thins between frames, and the segmentation threshold that worked at noon fails at six. Locking exposure, focus, and white balance manually was the single biggest improvement in scan quality we found. No assembly guide mentions it.

Turning the lights off is the second. Horus segments the laser by thresholding what the camera sees, and the easiest way to help it is to remove the competition. In a dark room the laser is the only red thing in the frame and background subtraction becomes trivial. The poster's "scanning in the dark" claim is really a description of the scanner working at its best.

Surfaces are the third. A red line laser fails on shiny, dark, and translucent objects. [Specular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specular_reflection) surfaces throw the line somewhere else, dark ones absorb it, and translucent ones glow from inside. The community fix is a light dusting of talcum powder, so a talc tin lived next to the scanner and everything difficult got scanned in matte white.

The mechanics show up in the data. A cheap turntable bearing has wobble, and the wobble shows up as a ripple around every scan at exactly the rotation frequency. We learned to read point clouds the way we had learned to read failed prints. The step angle is a real trade too. Finer steps mean denser slices and longer scans, which is where the poster's two-to-three-minute range comes from.

We also ran comparison experiments the kit doesn't ask for. One laser or two is a real question. The second laser halves occlusion shadows and doubles the calibration burden, and a slightly misaligned second plane ghosts every surface twice. The archive has a `dibba_singlelaser.ply` from the night we settled it. We tried [photogrammetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photogrammetry) on the side. The laser won on repeatability at this budget.

![The checkerboard calibration target standing on the turntable, the webcam and both laser mounts facing it](/images/posts/ciclop/calibration.jpg){: srcset="/images/posts/ciclop/calibration-480.jpg 480w, /images/posts/ciclop/calibration-960.jpg 960w, /images/posts/ciclop/calibration.jpg 1600w" sizes="(max-width: 800px) 92vw, 770px" loading="lazy" decoding="async"}

All of this sits on [calibration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_resectioning). The checkerboard calibrates the camera [intrinsics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_matrix), then the laser planes, then the turntable axis. The printed structure is really a jig for holding those three calibrations steady. Bump a laser mount and the hardware is fine while every scan is garbage.

## How Horus processes a scan

[Horus](https://github.com/bqlabs/horus) is a Python desktop application built on [OpenCV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenCV). It runs the machine over a serial line, using G-code-style commands to switch each laser and step the turntable one fixed angle per slice. For every slice the capture loop grabs a frame with the laser on and one with it off, subtracts the pair to remove the room, thresholds what's left in the red channel, and collapses the stripe to a per-row weighted centroid. That places the line's center between pixels, at a fraction of a pixel's width.

![Horus mid-scan on the laptop: the camera pane with the laser line on the left, the point cloud growing on the right](/images/posts/ciclop/software-view.jpg){: srcset="/images/posts/ciclop/software-view-480.jpg 480w, /images/posts/ciclop/software-view-960.jpg 960w, /images/posts/ciclop/software-view.jpg 1245w" sizes="(max-width: 800px) 92vw, 770px" loading="lazy" decoding="async"}

Every centroid then becomes geometry. The intrinsics from the checkerboard turn a pixel into a ray, the ray meets the calibrated laser plane at exactly one point, that intersection is a 3D coordinate in the camera's frame, and a rotation by the turntable's commanded angle adds it to the model. The whole scanner is three coordinate transforms run eight hundred times per revolution.

Our additions live around the edges of that loop. A camera script forces exposure, focus, and white balance to fixed values before every session, because segmentation constants are useless while the webcam keeps changing the image. Threshold presets per material class (matte, dark, and shiny-after-talc) saved retuning from scratch every night. And cleanup moved into meshlabserver, MeshLab's headless mode, so outlier removal, normal estimation, and Poisson reconstruction ran with identical parameters on every cloud. Two scans of the same object stayed comparable.

## Point cloud to STL

Horus exports [.ply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLY_(file_format)) point clouds, and clouds aren't printable. The pipeline runs through [MeshLab](https://www.meshlab.net/): statistical outlier removal for the stray points the threshold let through, [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_(geometry)) estimation, Poisson surface reconstruction to close the surface, then export to [STL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STL_(file_format)) for the printer. The file names in the scans folder show how it went, from `test_toi` and `first_deo` (a deodorant can) to the night one file got saved as `insane.ply`, which is what you name a scan when it finally works.

![The jar under the laser line beside its finished point cloud rendered from insane.ply](/images/posts/ciclop/scan-vs-object.jpg){: srcset="/images/posts/ciclop/scan-vs-object-480.jpg 480w, /images/posts/ciclop/scan-vs-object-960.jpg 960w, /images/posts/ciclop/scan-vs-object.jpg 1600w" sizes="(max-width: 800px) 92vw, 770px" loading="lazy" decoding="async"}

The bill for the whole machine came to about ₹14,000. The two lasers cost a hundred rupees, and the printed structure cost more than all the electronics combined. The parts came off the same class of machine the scanner now feeds, so the loop closes in both directions. The printer made the scanner, and the scanner makes files for the printer. Being able to copy an object off my desk, edit it on the way through, and print it, for less than a textbook, still feels a bit unreal.

## What we learned

1. Lock the camera before trusting the math. Every segmentation constant assumes yesterday's exposure, and an autoexposure webcam changes that every frame. The calibration is only as stable as the camera settings under it.
2. The shape of a defect names its cause. A double wall in the cloud is a misaligned second laser. Ripple at once per revolution is the bearing. Fuzz everywhere is the threshold.
3. Use the same parameters for every scan. With one meshlabserver script on every cloud, two scans of one object differ only by the scanner.
4. The coarsest step in the pipeline sets the resolution. Our 0.5 mm came from the pixel footprint at standoff distance. Polishing the subpixel centroid past that did nothing.
5. A festival crowd is a free abuse test. Two days of strangers spinning the turntable by hand found weaknesses two months of careful sessions never touched.
6. The STL files are open source. The part a repository can't hold is the folklore: which talc on which surface, which threshold for which evening light. Write that down too.
